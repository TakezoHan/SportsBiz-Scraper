Çiya Sofrası is still the most important restaurant in Istanbul.

Musa Dağdeviren's Kadıköy lokanta is not a restaurant so much as a living archive of Anatolian cooking — and it just happens to also serve the best ekşili ufak köfte you'll eat this year.

9.1/10 Kadıköy Anatolian Lunch ₺₺ Moderate

By Deniz Aksoy · Updated 12 April 2026 · Visited anonymously, four times in twelve months. Bill paid in full.

The crowd on Güneşlibahçe Sokak is the first thing you notice: a slow, multilingual shuffle of locals, diaspora Turks home for a wedding, Japanese cooks on pilgrimage, and at least one very serious-looking Danish food writer. They are all here for the same reason. Çiya isn't "good for a lokanta." Çiya is the reason the rest of Istanbul had to raise its game.

What it is

Three rooms — Çiya Sofrası (home-style), Çiya Kebap, and Çiya Kebapçısı — sit across from each other on the same narrow street behind the Kadıköy bazaar. Sofrası is the one you want. It's a bright, wood-panelled room built around a long steam table of some thirty-five dishes that rotate with the seasons and with whatever Musa's research team has dug up that week: a forgotten Gaziantep soup, a pickled walnut you have never seen before, a dolma the size of your thumb stuffed with sour grape leaves Musa found in a village of forty-two people.

You point. They weigh. You pay by the gram. This matters: it removes the single most Istanbul-style annoyance, the theatrical menu with no prices.

What to order

  • Ekşili ufak köfte — tiny meatballs in a hot-sour broth of dried plum and pomegranate. The house benchmark.
  • Analı kızlı çorba — "mother and daughter" soup from Gaziantep, two sizes of bulgur köfte in a yoghurt-spiked broth.
  • Whatever is on the far right of the steam table. That's where the research dishes land. Ask, and someone will tell you which village it came from.
  • A wedge of candied pumpkin with tahini and crushed walnut. Non-negotiable.
You leave Çiya believing two things at once: that Turkish cooking is the most under-documented cuisine on earth, and that someone is quietly documenting it anyway.

When to go

The trick with Çiya is not which day but which hour. Weekday lunch is the move — arrive between 11:30 and 12:15 or after 15:00. Friday and Saturday lunches will cost you 30–40 minutes on the street. Sundays are calmer than Saturdays. Dinner is fine but the steam table has been hunted over by then; you want the morning drop.

How we scored it

Cooking 9.6 · Hospitality 8.8 · Setting 8.4 · Value 9.5 · Come-back-Tuesday 9.4. Weighted, it lands at 9.1 / 10. We've eaten here four times in the last twelve months, anonymously, and paid in full each time. The score dropped a tenth from last year's update: staffing at peak hours has strained, and two of our visits saw a slightly tired evening steam table.
